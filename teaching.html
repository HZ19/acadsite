Teaching philosophy

Most students who struggle in a quantitative course aren't struggling with the math. They're struggling with notation — the gap between a formal expression on a slide and anything they can picture — and the recitation or lab section is where that gap usually closes or widens. I learned this running the first lab for a graduate causal inference course at EUI, where I set aside the derivations and built a simple simulation in R with the group, line by line, stopping at every step to look at the actual output.

I teach by writing code in front of students and having them write it themselves. Live coding makes the reasoning visible — every intermediate object can be inspected, every assumption has to be made explicit before a line will run, and mistakes happen in public and get diagnosed rather than hidden. I work derivations on the board at the pace the room can actually follow, and pair every formal step with the same point in plain language, because a student who can only reproduce a derivation hasn't yet internalized it. I take the same approach into office hours, where a student's confusion is usually easier to locate by asking them to run something than by asking them to describe the problem.

Weekly one-to-one tutorial teaching at Oxford taught me the same lesson from the other direction: how differently individual students arrive at the same difficulty, and how little a single explanation delivered to everyone at once tends to achieve. I treat a request to "go over it again" as diagnostic information — usually it means a student can't yet connect an estimator to the data in front of them, not that they need to hear the same derivation twice.

On AI tools: I use them in my own work for editing and data management, and the line I want students to draw is between using a model to get unstuck — legitimate, worth teaching properly — and using one to produce work they can't defend. That's a conversation I have explicitly in section, rather than leaving unsaid.
